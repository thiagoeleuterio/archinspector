# Rule ID

SOL-001

# Title

Decisions should address explicit requirements and constraints

# Category

Solution Architecture

# Status

Active

# Intent

Evaluate whether solution-level architectural decisions are grounded in explicit requirements and constraints that make the decision understandable, reviewable, and changeable.

# Applicability

Apply this rule when the reviewed material includes solution-level decisions about project or module organization, integration approach, deployment-relevant structure, technology selection, vendor or product choice, cross-cutting dependency structure, configuration placement, build-versus-buy direction, or other architecture choices that affect the solution as a whole.

The rule is relevant when there is enough evidence to identify a decision and the requirements, constraints, quality attributes, business needs, operational conditions, or trade-offs it is meant to address. Do not apply this rule to low-level implementation choices that have no solution-level architectural impact.

# Rule Statement

A solution-level architectural decision must be traceably connected to explicit requirements and constraints that justify the decision within the reviewed scope.

# Rationale

Solution architecture decisions often create long-lived structure, cost, operational commitments, integration dependencies, and limits on future change. When a decision is not tied to explicit requirements or constraints, reviewers cannot determine whether it is necessary, proportionate, or aligned with the problem being solved.

Traceable requirements and constraints make trade-offs visible and help separate intentional architecture from accidental structure. They also support later reconsideration when requirements, constraints, risks, or operating conditions change.

# Evidence Required

Evaluation requires traceable evidence identifying the solution-level decision, the requirement or constraint it addresses, and the relationship between them.

Direct evidence may include ADRs, decision records, solution architecture documents, requirement documents, quality attribute scenarios, constraints, risk records, trade-off analysis, dependency or topology diagrams with rationale, product or vendor selection rationale, cost or operational constraints, compliance constraints, stakeholder goals, and implementation or configuration evidence tied to the decision.

Evidence must distinguish explicit requirements and constraints from generic preference statements. Names, diagrams, repository layout, selected technology, framework defaults, or after-the-fact comments may support interpretation but must not be treated as conclusive evidence without rationale linked to the decision.

# Evaluation Guidance

First determine whether the reviewed item is a solution-level architectural decision. Then evaluate whether the decision has explicit requirements or constraints that explain why the selected approach is appropriate for the reviewed context.

Assess traceability rather than judging whether the decision is universally best. A decision can pass when the evidence shows a clear relationship to requirements and constraints, even if alternative solutions could also satisfy them. A decision should not pass merely because the technology or structure is common, modern, familiar, or already implemented.

Interpret evidence conservatively. Requirements and constraints may be documented in formal or lightweight form, but they must be specific enough to evaluate the decision. Vague claims such as scalability, simplicity, flexibility, best practice, or future-proofing are insufficient unless the reviewed material defines what they mean for the solution.

Keep adjacent responsibilities separate. This rule does not evaluate detailed Clean Architecture boundaries, Hexagonal ports, DDD models, SOLID object design, layered dependencies, messaging semantics, architecture test adequacy, or implementation correctness unless that evidence clarifies whether a solution-level decision is justified by explicit requirements and constraints.

# Pass Conditions

Use `Pass` when the reviewed scope is sufficient to identify the solution-level decision and evidence shows explicit requirements or constraints that the decision addresses.

Use `Pass` when the decision rationale explains the relevant trade-offs, quality attributes, business needs, operational constraints, integration constraints, compliance constraints, cost constraints, or delivery constraints well enough for a reviewer to evaluate fit within the reviewed scope.

Use `Pass` when implementation, configuration, or structural evidence is consistent with the stated decision and does not contradict the documented requirements or constraints.

# Fail Conditions

Use `Fail` when direct evidence shows a solution-level architectural decision with material impact but no explicit requirement, constraint, rationale, or trade-off evidence supporting why the decision exists.

Use `Fail` when the stated rationale is contradicted by reviewed requirements or constraints, when the decision addresses an unstated problem, or when the evidence shows the selected structure, technology, vendor, integration approach, or deployment-relevant choice is disconnected from the explicit drivers.

Use `Fail` when the only justification is generic preference, fashion, organizational habit, framework default, personal familiarity, or undocumented assumptions and the decision has architectural impact within the reviewed scope.

# Warning Conditions

Use `Warning` when evidence shows a partially justified decision, incomplete traceability, vague drivers, weak trade-off reasoning, or emerging mismatch between the decision and explicit requirements or constraints.

Use `Warning` when requirements or constraints exist but are too broad to fully justify the selected decision, when rationale is present but misses material trade-offs, or when implementation evidence is mostly consistent with the decision but leaves important ambiguity.

Use `Warning` when a decision appears reasonable from available evidence but the rationale depends on implicit context that is not fully documented or inspectable.

# Not Applicable Conditions

Use `Not Applicable` when the reviewed scope contains no solution-level architectural decisions relevant to this rule.

Use `Not Applicable` when the reviewed material concerns local implementation details, isolated code style, object-level design, domain modeling, messaging behavior, test mechanics, or architecture-style boundaries without a solution-level decision about the solution as a whole.

# Not Enough Evidence Conditions

Use `Not Enough Evidence` when the rule may be relevant but the reviewed material does not provide enough evidence to identify the decision, identify the requirements or constraints, inspect the rationale, evaluate traceability, or resolve conflicts between documentation and implementation.

Use `Not Enough Evidence` when only diagrams, repository structure, technology names, configuration fragments, meeting notes, or informal comments are available without enough context to determine the decision driver.

Use `Not Enough Evidence` when a decision is visible but the reviewed scope omits the requirements, constraints, ADRs, or stakeholder context needed to evaluate whether the decision is justified.

# Severity Guidance

The intended default severity for this rule is Medium. Assign higher severity when an unjustified or contradicted decision affects costly infrastructure, deployment topology, integration strategy, vendor lock-in, compliance posture, operational risk, cross-team dependencies, or broad solution structure.

Assign lower severity when the decision is localized, easily reversible, low cost, has limited architectural impact, or the missing rationale creates maintainability risk without immediate solution-level consequence. Severity must reflect architectural impact within the reviewed scope.

# Confidence Guidance

Use `Confirmed` when direct evidence clearly establishes the decision, the explicit requirements or constraints, and whether the decision is traceably connected to them.

Use `Likely` when multiple consistent evidence points support the evaluation but some rationale, stakeholder, implementation, or trade-off detail is unavailable. Use `Possible` when evidence is limited, indirect, partially documented, or structurally suggestive.

Use `Not Enough Evidence` when essential decision or requirement context is missing, documentation conflicts with implementation, or the reviewed material cannot show why the solution-level choice was made. Naming, technology selection, or structure alone must not produce `Confirmed` confidence.
