# Orion Commerce Platform

## Executive Summary

Orion Commerce Platform is a fictitious digital commerce system designed for a mid-sized retailer operating across web, mobile, and partner sales channels. The platform supports product discovery, customer account management, pricing, inventory visibility, checkout, payment capture, order orchestration, fulfillment coordination, shipping updates, and customer notifications.

The system evolved from a regional storefront into a broader commerce platform serving multiple countries, brands, and fulfillment models. It now combines a legacy monolithic commerce application, several newer domain services, shared databases, event-driven integrations, batch jobs, and third-party SaaS dependencies. Some parts are mature and operationally stable, while others were added quickly to support business deadlines and remain difficult to change safely.

The intended architectural review should assess whether Orion Commerce Platform can continue scaling toward higher transaction volume, broader international expansion, marketplace capabilities, stronger compliance requirements, and faster product delivery without excessive operational risk.

## Business Context

Orion Commerce Platform serves a retail organization that sells consumer electronics, accessories, warranties, and subscription services through direct-to-consumer channels. The company competes on product availability, personalized pricing, fast delivery options, reliable checkout, and post-purchase customer experience.

The main business problem is that growth has outpaced the architecture. Sales volume has increased, promotional campaigns generate unpredictable demand spikes, fulfillment is now distributed across warehouses and stores, and customer expectations for real-time information have risen. At the same time, engineering teams report slower delivery, fragile deployments, recurring incidents during peak traffic, and difficulty understanding ownership boundaries.

Business objectives include increasing conversion rate, reducing cart abandonment, improving order accuracy, enabling regional expansion, supporting differentiated pricing strategies, reducing operational incidents, accelerating experimentation, and lowering the cost of platform changes.

Key stakeholders include retail executives, digital commerce leadership, customer support, finance, warehouse operations, marketing, fraud operations, security and compliance, data analytics, external payment partners, shipping carriers, and five internal engineering teams responsible for the platform.

## Business Domains

Customer covers customer profiles, account preferences, saved addresses, loyalty status, communication preferences, customer segmentation, and support-related account metadata. The domain currently overlaps with Identity and Orders because customer records are stored in multiple locations and updated through several paths.

Catalog covers product master data, product variants, descriptions, media references, categories, merchandising attributes, search metadata, and regional assortment rules. Catalog data is imported from an ERP system and enriched manually by merchandising teams.

Inventory covers stock availability, reservations, warehouse quantities, store pickup eligibility, safety stock, backorder rules, and inventory reconciliation. The domain is business critical because inaccurate stock data directly causes overselling, cancellations, and customer support volume.

Pricing covers base prices, regional prices, customer-specific discounts, promotions, coupons, bundles, loyalty pricing, and price overrides. Pricing rules are complex and frequently changed by marketing and commercial teams.

Orders covers carts, checkout sessions, order submission, order state transitions, cancellations, returns initiation, order history, and downstream fulfillment coordination. The order lifecycle is spread across the legacy commerce application, the order service, and warehouse integrations.

Payments covers payment authorization, capture, refunds, payment method token references, fraud review, settlement reconciliation, and payment provider integrations. Sensitive payment details are delegated to external providers, but payment state and audit metadata are stored internally.

Shipping covers delivery options, carrier selection, shipping rate calculation, label requests, tracking updates, delivery promises, split shipments, and exception handling. Shipping integrates with carrier APIs and warehouse management systems.

Notification covers transactional email, SMS, push notifications, customer service alerts, operational alerts, and marketing-triggered communication handoffs. Some notifications are event-driven, while others are generated by scheduled jobs.

Identity covers authentication, account registration, password reset, MFA enrollment, session handling, service-to-service credentials, roles, and administrative access. Identity uses an external identity provider but still contains legacy authentication logic in the monolith.

## Functional Requirements

1. Customers must be able to browse products by category, search term, brand, price range, rating, and availability.
2. Customers must be able to view product details, including images, specifications, compatible accessories, price, stock status, and delivery options.
3. Customers must be able to create an account using email and password or supported social login providers.
4. Customers must be able to authenticate, maintain a session, reset passwords, and manage MFA where enabled.
5. Customers must be able to manage saved addresses, preferred payment methods, notification preferences, and profile information.
6. Customers must be able to add products to a cart, modify quantities, remove items, and save items for later.
7. The cart must validate product availability, purchasing limits, regional eligibility, and active pricing before checkout.
8. Customers must be able to apply coupons, promotion codes, loyalty benefits, and eligible bundle discounts.
9. The platform must calculate taxes, shipping costs, discounts, and final order totals during checkout.
10. Customers must be able to choose among available shipping, pickup, and split-delivery options.
11. Customers must be able to submit orders using credit card, digital wallet, store credit, or approved financing options.
12. The platform must authorize payment before final order confirmation.
13. The platform must create an order record with a unique order number after successful checkout.
14. The platform must reserve inventory for confirmed orders and release reservations when orders are cancelled or payment authorization fails.
15. Customers must receive order confirmation notifications after successful order placement.
16. Customers must be able to view current and historical orders from their account.
17. Customers must be able to track shipment status when carrier tracking information is available.
18. Customer support agents must be able to search for customers, carts, payments, and orders using administrative tools.
19. Customer support agents must be able to cancel eligible orders before shipment.
20. Customer support agents must be able to initiate refunds and returns according to policy.
21. Merchandising users must be able to update product descriptions, images, category placement, and featured product flags.
22. Commercial users must be able to configure promotions, coupons, price overrides, and campaign-specific pricing windows.
23. Inventory managers must be able to import warehouse stock updates and review reconciliation discrepancies.
24. Operations users must be able to monitor failed order submissions, payment exceptions, and shipping integration failures.
25. The platform must publish order-created, payment-captured, inventory-reserved, shipment-created, and order-cancelled events.
26. The platform must consume catalog, inventory, customer, and fulfillment updates from internal and external systems.
27. The platform must support regional storefront configuration for currency, language, tax rules, catalog assortment, and shipping options.
28. The platform must support guest checkout where permitted by regional policy.
29. The platform must detect likely duplicate order submissions and prevent duplicate payment capture.
30. The platform must expose reporting feeds for sales, refunds, promotion usage, inventory movement, and fulfillment performance.

## Non-Functional Requirements

1. Product search results should return within 500 ms at the 95th percentile under normal load.
2. Product detail pages should render required commerce data within 800 ms at the 95th percentile.
3. Checkout pricing and inventory validation should complete within 1 second at the 95th percentile.
4. Order submission should complete within 3 seconds at the 95th percentile excluding external payment provider latency.
5. The storefront should achieve 99.95% monthly availability.
6. Checkout and order submission should achieve 99.99% monthly availability during peak retail periods.
7. The platform should support at least 10x normal traffic during major promotional campaigns.
8. The architecture should allow independent scaling of catalog browsing, checkout, payment processing, and notification workloads.
9. The platform should tolerate temporary unavailability of notification providers without blocking order placement.
10. The platform should avoid overselling by maintaining reliable inventory reservation semantics.
11. Payment processing should be idempotent across retries and client resubmissions.
12. Customer data must be encrypted in transit and at rest.
13. Administrative access must require MFA and role-based authorization.
14. Service-to-service communication must use managed identities, signed tokens, or mutually authenticated channels.
15. The platform must comply with applicable PCI DSS obligations by avoiding storage of raw payment card data.
16. The platform must support GDPR and CCPA-style data access and deletion workflows where legally required.
17. Security-relevant events must be logged with enough detail to support audit and investigation.
18. Distributed tracing must cover checkout, payment, order creation, inventory reservation, and shipment handoff flows.
19. Business and technical metrics must be available for conversion, errors, latency, queue depth, retries, and external dependency health.
20. Teams must be able to deploy most domain services independently.
21. Rollbacks should be possible within 15 minutes for standard service deployments.
22. Database schema changes must support backward compatibility during rolling deployments.
23. The system should support blue-green or canary deployment for customer-facing services.
24. Cloud cost should be attributable by domain, environment, and major workload type.
25. Non-production environments should use cost controls, scheduled shutdowns, or reduced capacity where feasible.

## Constraints

- The company must continue operating the existing storefront during any modernization effort.
- The current ERP remains the system of record for product master data and financial accounting.
- The warehouse management system is operated by a third party and exposes limited integration options.
- Payment processing must continue using the current payment service provider for at least the next contract year.
- The business requires support for peak campaigns with dates set by marketing, leaving limited blackout windows.
- Some regional tax and invoicing rules are implemented in legacy code with limited documentation.
- Engineering teams are organized by a mix of technical layer and business domain, creating ownership ambiguity.
- The platform must run primarily on the approved cloud provider.
- Security review is required before introducing new customer-facing authentication flows.
- Data retention policies vary by region and must be approved by legal and compliance teams.
- The company has limited capacity for large data migrations during the current fiscal year.
- Customer support tools depend on direct read access to several existing operational databases.
- Some integrations require static IP allowlisting and cannot be changed quickly.
- The release process must support emergency fixes outside normal deployment windows.

## Assumptions

- The platform is expected to grow across additional countries and brands over the next two years.
- Web remains the primary revenue channel, but mobile traffic continues to increase.
- The current monolith cannot be fully retired during the v0.7.0 review horizon.
- External payment, identity, shipping, tax, and ERP systems remain outside direct engineering control.
- Product catalog size will continue to grow, including bundles, variants, and marketplace-like listings.
- Inventory accuracy is a primary business differentiator.
- Customer support volume is strongly correlated with failed checkout, delayed shipment, and unclear order state.
- Teams can change service boundaries over time if migration paths are incremental.
- Business teams will continue requiring frequent promotion and pricing changes.
- The architecture review should identify risks, trade-offs, coupling, operability gaps, and modernization opportunities.

## Existing Architecture

Orion Commerce Platform currently uses a hybrid architecture. The original commerce monolith still handles storefront rendering, cart management, checkout orchestration, customer account pages, promotion evaluation, and several administrative workflows. Over time, new services were added around the monolith for catalog search, payment orchestration, notification delivery, inventory synchronization, identity integration, and analytics exports.

The monolith uses a relational database that contains customers, carts, orders, promotions, product snapshots, and operational configuration. Several newer services also read directly from this database for convenience. Some services own separate databases, but ownership boundaries are inconsistent, and multiple teams occasionally update the same logical data through different mechanisms.

Catalog data originates in the ERP, flows through nightly batch jobs into the monolith database, and is then indexed into a search cluster. A newer catalog service exposes product APIs for mobile and partner channels, but the web storefront still uses monolith queries for some catalog views. This creates inconsistent product visibility when imports partially fail.

Inventory updates arrive from warehouses and stores through a message broker. The inventory service maintains near-real-time stock views and publishes availability events. However, checkout still calls a legacy inventory reservation endpoint hosted in the monolith, which sometimes uses stale cached stock data. Manual inventory corrections are performed through database scripts during incident response.

Pricing is split between a legacy promotion engine inside the monolith and a newer pricing service used by mobile clients. The two engines share some rule definitions but evaluate them differently. Marketing teams can configure overlapping campaigns without a single validation workflow, causing occasional price mismatches between cart, product detail, and final checkout.

Order placement is synchronous from storefront to monolith, then partly asynchronous after order creation. The monolith creates the initial order, calls the payment service, writes payment state back to its own database, reserves inventory, and emits an order-created event. Downstream fulfillment, notification, analytics, and fraud processes subscribe to events. Event schemas are not consistently versioned.

The payment service is comparatively well isolated and delegates sensitive card data to the payment provider. It supports idempotency keys for authorization and capture, but the monolith does not always propagate stable keys during retry scenarios. Refunds are handled through a support console that calls payment APIs directly and later reconciles with order state.

Notifications are processed through a notification service connected to email, SMS, and push providers. Some transactional notifications are event-driven, but others are sent by batch jobs scanning order tables. This creates duplicate notification risk and makes it difficult to trace customer communication history.

Authentication is delegated to an external identity provider for new web and mobile flows. Legacy admin screens still use local authentication tables in the monolith. Session handling differs between storefront, support tools, and internal admin interfaces.

Deployment is partially automated. Newer services use container images and pipeline-based deployments. The monolith is deployed as a large application package with manual approvals, database migration scripts, and environment-specific configuration files. Production deployments are usually weekly, but hotfixes are frequent during campaigns.

The architecture has some strong foundations, including service-specific ownership for payments and notifications, use of managed cloud infrastructure, partial event-driven integration, centralized logging, and externalized authentication for customer-facing flows. It also contains deliberate imperfections such as shared databases, inconsistent service boundaries, duplicated business logic, mixed synchronous and asynchronous state transitions, weak schema governance, incomplete observability, and brittle release practices.

## Technology Stack

Backend: Java 11 Spring Boot for newer services, Java 8 Spring MVC for the monolith, Node.js for selected integration workers, and Python scripts for operational batch tasks.

Frontend: React for newer account and checkout experiments, server-side rendered JSP pages in the monolith, a separate React Native mobile application, and internal admin screens built with mixed legacy templates.

Database: PostgreSQL for service databases, a large shared Oracle database for the monolith, Redis for caching and session data, and Elasticsearch/OpenSearch for product search.

Messaging: Kafka for domain events, RabbitMQ for some older integration queues, and scheduled file-based transfers for ERP and finance integrations.

Cloud: Kubernetes on the approved cloud provider, managed PostgreSQL, managed Redis, object storage, CDN, load balancers, secrets manager, and serverless jobs for lightweight scheduled processing.

CI/CD: GitHub Actions for newer services, Jenkins for the monolith, manual production approval gates, Helm charts for Kubernetes deployments, and database migration scripts managed inconsistently across teams.

Observability: Centralized log aggregation, Prometheus metrics for newer services, Grafana dashboards, partial OpenTelemetry tracing, uptime checks, and manual business reconciliation reports.

Authentication: External identity provider for customers, OAuth2/OIDC for new services, legacy username and password tables for internal admin screens, API keys for some partner integrations, and static credentials for older batch jobs.

Storage: Cloud object storage for product media, invoices, exports, and integration files; local disk usage remains in the monolith for temporary batch processing and generated reports.

## Deployment Topology

Development uses local developer machines, shared development databases, lightweight service containers, mocked payment provider endpoints, and a shared Kafka cluster. Developers can run some services independently, but the monolith often requires access to shared infrastructure.

QA uses a persistent integrated environment with reduced-size databases, test payment credentials, nightly catalog imports, shared test accounts, and partially automated regression suites. QA data is frequently refreshed but not always aligned with production-like scenarios.

Staging is intended to mirror production topology but runs at smaller capacity. It includes the monolith, domain services, managed databases, message brokers, CDN configuration, and third-party sandbox integrations. Staging is also used for business validation, performance testing, and release rehearsals, which sometimes causes environment contention.

Production runs across multiple availability zones for customer-facing traffic. Newer services are deployed to Kubernetes with horizontal scaling policies. The monolith runs on a smaller pool of managed virtual machines behind a load balancer. Databases are managed services where possible, but the monolith database has special operational procedures. Messaging clusters support event-driven flows, while some batch integrations still rely on scheduled file exchange.

## Teams

Commerce Core owns the monolith, cart, checkout orchestration, order creation, and several legacy administrative workflows. The team has deep operational knowledge but carries a large maintenance burden.

Customer Experience owns web frontend experiences, mobile API coordination, account pages, customer profile features, and selected personalization capabilities.

Product and Pricing owns catalog APIs, search integration, merchandising workflows, pricing service, promotion configuration, and product data quality.

Fulfillment and Payments owns payment orchestration, fraud integration, inventory reservation, warehouse handoff, shipping coordination, refunds, and returns initiation.

Platform Operations owns cloud infrastructure, CI/CD templates, observability, security tooling, runtime reliability practices, shared messaging platforms, and production incident coordination.

## Architecture Decisions

1. Keep the monolith operational while extracting selected capabilities incrementally.
2. Use managed Kubernetes for newer backend services.
3. Continue using the Oracle monolith database as the operational source for legacy checkout and order flows.
4. Allow newer services to read selected monolith tables directly for short-term delivery speed.
5. Use PostgreSQL as the default database for new domain services.
6. Use Kafka as the strategic event backbone for domain events.
7. Continue using RabbitMQ for older fulfillment integrations until partner contracts are revised.
8. Use Redis for cart cache, session cache, and selected product availability caching.
9. Use an external identity provider for customer authentication.
10. Preserve legacy admin authentication inside the monolith until internal tools are replaced.
11. Maintain a separate payment service to isolate payment provider integration.
12. Store payment tokens and transaction references, but not raw card data.
13. Let the monolith orchestrate checkout synchronously for web traffic.
14. Let mobile clients call the pricing service directly for product and cart estimates.
15. Use separate pricing logic paths for web and mobile until a unified pricing engine is funded.
16. Publish order events after database commit through application code rather than a transactional outbox.
17. Use search indexing for catalog browsing instead of querying the ERP directly.
18. Perform catalog imports nightly, with limited support for urgent manual corrections.
19. Use blue-green deployment for selected stateless services.
20. Deploy the monolith weekly with manual approval and coordinated database migration scripts.
21. Keep support reporting queries connected directly to production replicas.
22. Centralize logs across services but allow teams to define metrics independently.

## Known Technical Debt

1. The monolith contains cart, checkout, order, customer, promotion, and admin logic in tightly coupled modules.
2. Several services read directly from the monolith database instead of using stable APIs or events.
3. Payment idempotency is inconsistently implemented across web checkout retries.
4. Promotion rules are duplicated between the monolith and pricing service.
5. Inventory reservations depend on legacy cache behavior that is poorly documented.
6. Event schemas are not consistently versioned or validated.
7. Some services publish events before downstream state is fully durable.
8. Database migration scripts are manually ordered for monolith releases.
9. Test coverage for checkout edge cases is incomplete.
10. End-to-end tests are slow, flaky, and dependent on shared QA data.
11. Operational batch scripts are written in inconsistent styles and lack ownership metadata.
12. Some secrets are rotated manually and documented outside the standard secrets manager.
13. Legacy admin screens use outdated authentication and authorization patterns.
14. Customer deletion requests require manual coordination across multiple databases.
15. Product import failures require database-level remediation.
16. Support tools rely on direct SQL queries with limited access control.
17. Shipping carrier integrations have inconsistent retry and timeout behavior.
18. Notification templates are stored in multiple systems with no single approval workflow.
19. Application configuration differs between environments and is not fully declarative.
20. Some services lack resource limits and autoscaling policies.
21. Observability dashboards are uneven across domains.
22. Alert thresholds are mostly infrastructure-focused rather than customer-journey-focused.
23. Local development requires access to shared services that are frequently unstable.
24. Dependency versions differ significantly between services.
25. Performance tests are run manually before major campaigns but not as part of routine delivery.

## Known Architectural Smells

1. Shared database access blurs service ownership boundaries.
2. The monolith remains the implicit orchestrator for too many business processes.
3. Checkout combines pricing, inventory, payment, customer, tax, and order concerns in one synchronous path.
4. Business rules for pricing and promotions are duplicated across channels.
5. Inventory state is cached in multiple places with unclear freshness guarantees.
6. Some asynchronous flows lack reliable reconciliation.
7. Event publishing is not transactionally coupled to state changes.
8. Event consumers depend on undocumented payload fields.
9. Service boundaries reflect project history more than stable business capabilities.
10. Support tooling bypasses domain APIs and reads operational data directly.
11. Manual database fixes are part of normal incident response.
12. Legacy and modern authentication mechanisms coexist without a clear retirement plan.
13. Environment parity is incomplete, especially between staging and production.
14. The deployment model encourages large coordinated releases for monolith changes.
15. Observability is stronger at the service level than at the business process level.
16. Failure handling differs across payment, shipping, notification, and fulfillment integrations.
17. Batch jobs and event consumers sometimes update the same records.
18. There is no single authoritative order state machine documented across systems.
19. Regional rules are embedded in application code instead of managed as explicit policy.
20. Cross-team ownership of customer data is ambiguous.
21. APIs expose internal data shapes that are difficult to evolve.
22. Some services use synchronous calls where eventual consistency would be acceptable.
23. Some critical workflows rely on eventual consistency without compensating controls.
24. Reporting workloads compete with operational workloads on shared replicas.
25. Architecture documentation lags behind implementation changes.

## Future Roadmap

Phase 1 focuses on architectural visibility and risk reduction. The platform should document current domain ownership, critical data flows, order state transitions, integration dependencies, operational runbooks, and customer-impacting failure modes. Teams should establish common standards for event schema versioning, idempotency, observability, and service ownership.

Phase 2 focuses on checkout stabilization. The platform should improve payment idempotency, introduce a reliable outbox or equivalent event publication mechanism, consolidate order state handling, reduce direct database dependencies, and define clear reconciliation flows for payment, inventory, shipping, and notification failures.

Phase 3 focuses on domain separation. The platform should move toward clearer ownership of Customer, Catalog, Pricing, Inventory, Orders, Payments, Shipping, Notification, and Identity capabilities. Legacy database access should be replaced gradually with APIs, events, or replicated read models owned by the appropriate teams.

Phase 4 focuses on modernization of customer-facing capabilities. The platform should unify pricing behavior across web and mobile, migrate account and checkout experiences away from legacy rendering where appropriate, improve regional configuration, and strengthen experimentation capabilities without compromising checkout reliability.

Phase 5 focuses on scale, compliance, and operational excellence. The platform should support higher campaign traffic, stronger privacy workflows, consistent deployment automation, cost attribution, production-like performance testing, business-level observability, and controlled retirement of remaining legacy authentication and batch-processing patterns.
